Plain Language Summary

A management consulting firm called Kearney — and a closely connected lobbying shop called The Cohen Group — have built a system where retired U.S. generals and senior defense officials use the relationships they built on the public's dime to make money for private clients. Those clients include defense companies competing for government contracts, and foreign governments the U.S. is supposed to be dealing with at arm's length.

Most of it is legal. Some of it probably isn't. One person in the broader network already went to prison for doing explicitly what the rest do structurally.

Imagine a building inspector who spends 30 years deciding whether construction projects pass code. The day he retires, he goes to work for a construction company — telling them how to pass the inspections he used to run. Federal law says he can't personally call his old colleagues for two years. After that, he's free. Now scale that up to four-star generals and $355 million defense contracts.

Adjacent Entity

The Cohen Group — Highest-Risk Node

The Cohen Group: What It Is

William S. Cohen: 18-year Senate Armed Services Committee member, co-authored Goldwater-Nichols, Nunn-Cohen (USSOCOM), Clinger-Cohen (federal IT procurement). Secretary of Defense 1997–2001. Founded The Cohen Group within weeks of leaving office.

Current senior counselors include the former SACEUR (Scaparrotti), the former Secretary of Defense (Mattis), and the former VCJCS (Ralston) — who simultaneously served as a registered Senate lobbyist for Lockheed Martin while acting as U.S. Special Envoy to counter the PKK in Turkey, a country that was buying $13 billion in Lockheed aircraft at the same time.

The Saudi Arabia office opened September 12, 2018. Jamal Khashoggi was murdered October 2, 2018. Every major competitor publicly exited Saudi relationships in the weeks that followed. The Cohen Group did not. The office is still listed as active. No FARA registration has been filed.

Bottom Line

This is not a story about a few bad actors. 81% of four-star officers who retired between 2018 and 2023 went directly to work for the arms industry.

The admiral convicted in May 2025 was not doing something different. He was doing the same thing — accepting future employment in exchange for directing government resources to a specific company. The difference is that he made the arrangement explicit while still in office, which crossed from "revolving door" into "bribery." Everyone else in this report negotiated the same outcome through the legally approved sequence: serve, retire, wait the minimum, take the job.

Three of those arrangements show documented facts, named individuals, and timelines strong enough to support a referral today. They are identified above.
